How to practise spelling words without tears

A ten-minute routine for the weekly spelling list that teaches the words before it tests them, and keeps the kitchen table a friendly place.

The weekly spelling list is the bit of homework most likely to end in tears, for children and for parents. It doesn't have to. Below is a ten-minute routine built on sounds rather than rote copying: it teaches each word before it tests it. It's written for Year 1 and Year 2, but it works with any list.

Why spelling practice turns into a fight

  • Repetition with no feedback. Writing a word ten times feels like work, and if the first one was wrong, the other nine practise the mistake.
  • Words that look random. To a six-year-old, s-a-i-d is just a string of letters. Without a way to see why a word is spelled the way it is, every word is a fresh memory test.
  • Testing before teaching. It's tempting to start with “spell ship for me”. But being asked to spell a word nobody has shown you how to build is a test, not practice, and getting it wrong at the kitchen table on Monday feels just as bad as getting it wrong on Friday.

The 10-minute routine

Do it at the same time each day, before everyone's tired. Take one word at a time; three or four words a night is plenty. The example word is ship, from week 3 of our Year 1 spelling list.

  1. Hear and say (1 minute). Say the word clearly, use it in a sentence, and have your child say it back. With ship: “The big ship sailed away. Ship.” Check they're saying the word they'll need to write.
  2. Sound it out by phonograms (3 minutes). Say the word slowly and count the sounds on your fingers: /sh/ /ĭ/ /p/, three sounds. Then match each sound to its phonogram: ship Four letters, three sounds, and the sh is one team.
  3. Write, cover, check (3 minutes). Your child says the sounds aloud as they write the word, then covers it and writes it again from memory. Uncover it and check together, one phonogram at a time, so a mistake lands on one sound rather than the whole word.
  4. Dictation sentence (2 minutes). Say a short sentence that uses the word plus one or two easy words they know, and have them write all of it: “I can see a ship.” This is where spelling meets real writing, which is the whole point of the list.
  5. Celebrate (1 minute). Read back what they wrote, point to one specific thing they got right (“you remembered sh is one sound”), and stop. Ending on a win makes tomorrow's ten minutes easier to start.

What to do when they get it wrong

Resist the urge to have them write it out ten times. Instead, put their attempt next to the correct spelling and find the phonogram that slipped. If they wrote shp for ship, the sh and the p were right; only the vowel went missing. Say the middle sound together, /ĭ/, write the word once more, and move on.

Most mistakes cluster in one place: a vowel, a letter team written letter by letter (sip for ship), or the odd bit in a tricky word. Once you know where the problem is, practice gets short and specific. For the words that really do break the rules, like said and was, see our guide to common exception words.

Spread it across the week

Most lists come home on Monday with a test on Friday. Split the list so no single night is heavy.

DayFocusTen minutes
MondayMeet the listRead all the words together and spot the sound they share. Full routine on the first three or four words.
TuesdayNext wordsFull routine on the next three or four, plus a quick look back at Monday's.
WednesdayLast words and tricky onesFinish the list, then give any tricky words the find-the-odd-bit treatment.
ThursdayPractice testSay each word in a sentence while your child writes it. Mark together and re-teach only the phonograms that slipped.
FridayTest at schoolNothing at home. Ask which word they were proudest of.

Signs it's working

  • Your child starts splitting new words into sounds without being asked.
  • Mistakes get smaller: one phonogram wrong rather than a whole word guessed.
  • Words from earlier weeks turn up spelled correctly in their own writing.
  • The routine takes less nagging to start, and some nights they ask for it.
  • Friday's result matters less to them, and Monday's list stops feeling like a mountain.

If the Year 1 phonics check is coming up in Term 3, the same sound-by-sound habit is exactly what it rewards.

Questions parents ask

How many times a week should we practise spelling?

Four short sessions, Monday to Thursday, is plenty for most Year 1 and 2 children. Ten minutes is the target; stop sooner if it's going well or badly. Short and regular beats one long session the night before the test.

Should I give a spelling test at home?

A quick, low-key one on Thursday is useful, because it shows you which words still wobble. Keep it to the week's words, say each one in a sentence, and mark it together. Treat a wrong answer as information about which phonogram slipped, not as a score.

My child cries at spelling homework. What now?

Stop for the night, and keep it short and light for a week: one or two words, with praise for effort. If the tears keep coming, have a chat with the teacher. They see your child learning every day, can tell you whether the list is pitched right, and may be happy for you to do fewer words. You don't have to push through it alone.

What age is this routine for?

It's written for Year 1 and Year 2, roughly ages 6 to 8, when weekly lists usually start. The same steps work for Prep or Kindy children with three or four simple words, and for older children with longer lists.
